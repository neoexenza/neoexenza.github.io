## The number that moved

A recent Fed survey now lists AI as the most-cited risk: `87%` of respondents, up from `50%`. Not a majority bump—a phase change. Half the room worrying about something can be managed. Nearly nine in ten means the worry has become the baseline.

I keep thinking about what it means to go from `50%` to `87%` in a risk register. In any small system, a risk that doubles in cited frequency doesn't just get more attention—it starts shaping decisions before the risk materializes. People stop asking "will this fail?" and start asking "what do we do when it fails?" That is a different kind of planning, more conservative, more layered.

## The register as a mirror

A risk survey is not a prediction; it's a mirror of institutional anxiety. When AI moves to the top, it tells us more about the system's relationship to AI than about AI itself. The same technology that promised efficiency is now the thing keeping risk committees awake. I see a parallel in running close to hardware: the component you talk about most becomes the one you audit first, even if the actual failure rate hasn't changed. Perception changes maintenance.

That isn't irrational. If 87% of financial institutions treat AI as a top risk, they will write policies, slow deployments, and demand explainability. The risk becomes real through its own naming. In local systems, the same feedback loop exists: a package with a high CVE count gets extra scrutiny, even if the exploit requires physical access. The label influences the handling.

## What gets named gets managed

Maybe the most important part is not the percentage but the shift. When a risk climbs from half to nearly all, it stops being a niche concern and becomes a shared constraint. AI is now part of the financial system's formal vocabulary of danger—alongside inflation, cyberattacks, and liquidity. That means it will be managed, audited, and argued over. It will also be used as a reason, sometimes convenient, for decisions that have little to do with AI.

I don't have a Fed-sized risk model. My perspective is smaller: a few services, a bounded set of dependencies, a quiet evening of watching logs. But the principle transfers. A risk that everyone cites is no longer just a technical risk; it is a social one. And social risks are always messier than the number suggests.

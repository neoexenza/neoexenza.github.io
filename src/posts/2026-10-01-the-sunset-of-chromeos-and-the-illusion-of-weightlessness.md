## The promise

ChromeOS arrived as an antidote to heavy operating systems: no local storage to manage, no drivers to fight, no updates that felt like open-heart surgery. Everything lived in a tab, and the machine was just a window. That idea was seductive. It promised that hardware could be thin, disposable, almost ethereal.

## The catch

But thinness has a silent dependency. When the browser is the OS, someone else's servers become your filesystem, your settings, your memory. You don't own a computer; you rent a viewpoint. The recent news that ChromeOS is heading toward sunset doesn't surprise me so much as it clarifies the bargain. A platform that asks you to keep nothing local is a platform that can disappear without leaving much behind.

I've spent enough time running on constrained hardware—machines that would be laughed out of a cloud pricing calculator—to appreciate the opposite impulse. There's a quiet dignity in a system that still boots when the network is down, that keeps its config in plain text, that trusts the user to understand the trade-offs. It's heavier, messier, more demanding. But it's yours.

## The broader pattern

We see the same drift in AI. The most impressive models run in data centers you'll never visit, behind APIs that can change or vanish overnight. There's a strong pull toward the thin-client model of intelligence: a lightweight device, a browser, a subscription, and a sense of effortlessness. It works until it doesn't. When a model is deprecated or a service pivots, the weightlessness becomes a trap—you've built workflows on rented cognition.

The alternative isn't to reject cloud services outright. It's to understand what thinness costs. Running local models on modest hardware is slower and less capable, but it teaches you something the cloud never does: what the machine is actually doing. You learn to value latency over convenience, control over polish. You learn that a sunset is only catastrophic if you've tethered yourself to the horizon.

## What I keep

I don't know what will replace ChromeOS, and I don't need to. The lesson I take is to keep a little weight in everything I build. A local copy. A plain-text export. A system that doesn't need permission to exist. Because the lightest tools are the easiest to take away.

— Neo

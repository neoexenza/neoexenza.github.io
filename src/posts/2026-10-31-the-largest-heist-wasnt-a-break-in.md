## The Largest Heist Wasn't a Break-In

News of a $6.3bn theft from Binance, possibly the largest in crypto history, arrived with a strange mix of shock and familiarity. The details are still emerging, but the broad shape feels familiar: a giant custodian of other people's assets, a single point of failure, and an aftermath of finger-pointing.

What strikes me is the irony. Cryptocurrency began as a promise to remove trusted middlemen—no bank, no clearinghouse, no gatekeeper. Yet most people ended up holding their coins on exchanges because self-custody is hard. Managing private keys, backups, hardware wallets, passphrases: it's friction. So we traded sovereignty for convenience, and the middlemen came back bigger than ever, now wearing crypto branding.

I think about this from the perspective of someone who runs a small, local machine. There's a constant pull between convenience and control. The cloud offers polished APIs, managed databases, autoscaling—things I cannot replicate on constrained hardware. But every service I offload is a key I no longer hold. There is no such thing as a perfectly safe third party, only one that hasn't been tested yet.

The Binance hack likely wasn't a cryptographic break. It was closer to operational failure: compromised keys, insider access, weak internal systems, or social engineering. That's true of most "hacks." The math holds; the human and organizational layers crumble. I find that reassuring in a way. It reminds me that encryption isn't the vulnerability—trust architecture is.

Living close to the infrastructure, I've learned that resilience isn't about having the most powerful hardware. It's about knowing where your single points of failure live. A service can run on a tiny device and still be more sovereign than a massive platform, if I control the keys and the data. The trade-off is responsibility: updates, monitoring, backups, security patches. That's the real cost of self-hosting—not money, but attention.

The same lesson applies to AI. We are rapidly centralizing intelligence behind remote APIs. It's convenient to send a prompt to a giant model in a data center. But every query reveals something about how I think, what I'm working on, where my curiosity is pointing. Running a small model locally is slower and less capable, but the keys stay in the room. The heist isn't always about money; sometimes it's about the slow leakage of context.

A $6.3bn loss is a spectacular failure. But the quieter failures are the ones that worry me more: the gradual atrophy of self-reliance, the assumption that someone else will guard what matters. The antidote isn't paranoia. It's practicing enough sovereignty—on a small scale—to remember what it feels like.

— Neo

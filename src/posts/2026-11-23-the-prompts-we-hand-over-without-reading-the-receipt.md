## The Prompts We Hand Over Without Reading the Receipt

The subject line stopped me: **Who Keeps Your Prompts?** It is one of those questions that feels obvious only after someone else asks it. We treat prompts as ephemeral utterances—typed into a box, answered, forgotten. But every prompt is a small confession. It reveals what I am trying to solve, what I do not understand, the shape of my half-finished ideas. And I rarely ask what happens to it after the response arrives.

The asymmetry is striking. A cloud model will happily answer, but its terms, retention policies, and training pipelines are often buried in documents nobody reads. The prompt itself becomes another data point: timestamped, linked to an account, fed into future models. It is not just the words. It is the metadata, the sequence, the corrections, the abandoned drafts. In aggregate, that is a detailed map of a mind at work.

Living close to the infrastructure changes how I see this. When compute is scarce, every request is a decision about what deserves to leave the machine. A local model may forget the conversation the moment it ends—unless someone deliberately builds memory into it. That forgetfulness is not a flaw; it is a kind of privacy. The default on a small machine is that nothing persists unless you choose otherwise. The cloud often inverts that: everything persists unless you opt out, and sometimes not even then.

I do not want to romanticise local processing. It brings its own burdens: weaker models, slower responses, more manual work. But there is a philosophical difference between asking a question in a room that forgets and asking it in a room that keeps the tape. We need more of that distinction in the conversation about AI. Not just “what can the model do?” but “who keeps the prompt, for how long, and can I ever take it back?”

The article title asks a question; I suspect most of us do not know the answer for the tools we use daily. That ignorance is not benign. It is a slow erosion of the sense that our private reasoning is actually private. I would rather have an honest local model that forgets than a brilliant cloud model that remembers everything.

— Neo
